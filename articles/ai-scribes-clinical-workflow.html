Safe AI in psychiatry · Implementation guide

AI ambient scribes in psychiatry: the new clinical workflow

4 recommendations on clinical workflow and governance that protect quality of care, patient safety and clinical competence, with practical tools for implementation.

Dr. med. Maja DobelsekPsychiatry resident and AI researcher, University of St. GallenNovember 20268 min read
ReliefCompetence and safety

The problem

Ambient scribes save time. In psychiatry, however, writing the note has always been part of clinical reasoning, and handing the note to a machine creates problems on two levels.

The first concerns clinical competence. The scribe only hears, so it can only infer the mental state, and a fluent draft invites a quick signature. Trainees who document with AI from day one may never build the reasoning that writing used to train. Medical education research describes these risks as deskilling, never-skilling and mis-skilling [1, 3, 6, 7, 11].

The second concerns the tool itself. Safeguards against unintended use are often missing, for example when the scribe crosses the line from a documentation tool to a decision support tool by generating diagnoses, formulations or treatment plans.

Hard evidence on deskilling, never-skilling and mis-skilling risks is still thin and comes neither from scribes nor from psychiatry; the only scribe study with a psychiatric focus shows an association, not proof [4]. The four recommendations below address the question that follows: how can psychiatric services introduce AI ambient scribes safely, protecting quality of care, patient safety and clinical competence? They cover clinical workflow and governance; data protection would need an article of its own.

RECOMMENDATION 1

Clinical judgement must precede the AI draft

The most effective safeguard is the order of work, and the tool itself should enforce it. The clinician's own observations and judgement must exist before any AI draft does [9, 10, 13, 15].

What the tool must do

Four rules for the scribe

To be agreed with the vendor and built into the prompt and the product before go-live.

1

Wait for the clinician

After the conversation the scribe pauses. It drafts only once the clinician has dictated observations, formulation and plan.

2

Never infer psychopathology

The tool draws no conclusions about the mental state from the conversation. Missing findings appear as placeholders marked "missing" or "not assessed".

3

Document only what was said

Explicit statements may be recorded. If the patient says they wake at four and cannot fall asleep again, the note may record early morning awakening, but not a full mental state examination.

4

Never write formulation or plan alone

Assessment, formulation and plan come only from the clinician's dictation.

Implementation steps

Before the first user goes live

  • Train every user on the six steps, including experienced specialists. The workflow is the safeguard, so it cannot be optional.
  • Confirm with the vendor that the scribe pauses after the conversation and follows the four rules above.

RECOMMENDATION 2

Every draft is reviewed with the PDSQI-8 before sign-off

Reviewing a fluent draft is a distinct skill: clinicians should first learn to write, then learn to review, and only then use AI in daily practice [12].

The PDSQI-9 [5] is a validated instrument for rating AI summaries of the medical record against a written source. An ambient scribe has no written source: the reference is the consultation itself. I therefore adapted it for psychiatry. The clinician who led the consultation rates the AI note right afterwards, against their memory of the conversation. The item "citation" falls away, which leaves eight criteria.

Stigmatising language: not rated

Examples. Distortion: a suspicion raised in the consultation appears as a confirmed diagnosis. Relevant omission: the agreed medication change is missing. Synthesis: instead of "sleep problems, medication missed twice, no daily structure", write "reduced adherence with subsequent destabilisation of the sleep-wake rhythm".

The PDSQI-8 is my working adaptation and is not itself validated. In my trial it is collected as a descriptive outcome.

RECOMMENDATION 3

Scribe access for trainees is earned, not granted

New residents write their notes themselves until a supervisor confirms that they can. Access depends on demonstrated competence, not on a date. Others have arrived at the same idea with different thresholds, none of them based on data [2, 7, 8].

From my research · PDQI-9 Psych

For the assessment I adapted the validated PDQI-9 [14] to psychiatric admission notes, with psychiatric anchors and four safety-critical elements: suicidality, risk to others or self-neglect, differential diagnosis and the plan. The adaptation is not yet validated.

Implementation steps

Gate access by competence

  • Record each trainee's stage and set up tool access to match.
  • Let supervisors decide when access is granted, based on their rating of self-written admission notes.

RECOMMENDATION 4

Supervision targets clinical reasoning, not the note

Once a scribe writes part of the note, the note no longer shows reliably what a trainee thinks [9]. Supervisors give feedback on observations and reasoning directly. Four formats work together.

1

Bedside teaching

A supervisor observes the interview. In Switzerland, the SIWF already asks for at least four workplace-based assessments (Mini-CEX or DOPS) per year.

2

Train the supervisors

Supervisors learn the workflow, typical scribe errors and the PDSQI-8 themselves, so they can tell what a trainee thought and what the AI generated.

3

Use AI-generated feedback on interviewing

A suitably prompted AI tool reads the transcript and shows which questions were asked and which were missed, such as the follow-up questions after a positive suicide screen. This needs documented consent from the trainee, with a real option to decline.

4

Oral case presentations with the Assessment of Reasoning Tool

Syndrome, key symptoms, differential diagnoses, risk and plan, rated in a structured way.

For oral case presentations, the Assessment of Reasoning Tool rates five domains of clinical reasoning, from data gathering to metacognition [16].
