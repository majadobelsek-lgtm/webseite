How the work of a psychiatrist will change once AI tools are everywhere

Many people assume that AI in psychiatry means less human contact. I expect the opposite.

Dr. med. Maja DobelsekPsychiatrist · PhD candidate, HSG 8 November 2026 · 3 min read
Personal perspective. This essay reflects my own view as a clinician and researcher, not a settled truth and not the position of my employer.

Once AI tools are part of everyday psychiatric work, the human part of our job will not shrink – it will become the job.

First, we will have to understand our tools

Whoever works with a tool has to understand how it works. This has always been true in medicine, and the tools we are about to work with are AI tools. Psychiatrists – like every medical professional – will therefore need a basic education in machine learning, neural networks and large language models: not to build them, but to know what they can and cannot do, where their outputs come from, and how to interpret their findings. This belongs in the university curriculum, next to pharmacology and psychopathology. A clinician who cannot read the tool cannot take responsibility for what the tool produces.

The diagnosis will no longer be ours alone

The real change is this: psychiatrists will no longer be the ones who set the diagnosis on the basis of their interview. A large part of the initial assessment will be done by AI – and AI has one advantage no clinician has: it can see many patients at the same time. Capacity grows massively. After that first assessment, triage can point each person to the care that fits them best: a psychiatric nurse, a social worker, a psychologist, primary care – or a psychiatrist. As highly specialised professionals, we will see only a fraction of the people who enter the system: those who would actually benefit from a psychiatrist rather than from another professional.

What remains is the human encounter

Interpretation and communication will become the core skills – critical interpretation of findings, nonviolent communication, empathy, emotional intelligence, the ability to build a working relationship, and the clinical judgement to choose the right tool and the right treatment.

And one thing will not change at all: the presence of another human body with its own consciousness that directs its attention and awareness towards another human being is an essential ingredient in treating mental health conditions. No AI can replace it.

Why the psychiatrist becomes a consultant

The analogy to a business consultant is more than a metaphor. Mental illness is not the result of a chemical imbalance in the brain. It emerges from the whole bio-psycho-social picture, in which the environment plays a major role. As in business and entrepreneurship, we cannot measure or capture every factor that contributes to the outcome. We work with probabilities, with best practices and – yes – with a gut feeling. Success depends a great deal on management skills: setting priorities, allocating resources, deciding under uncertainty, and carrying responsibility. This is the psychiatry we already practice today, but it will become even more obvious in the future.

Understanding the tools, mastering the encounter, deciding under uncertainty – that is the psychiatrist's job once AI is everywhere.
